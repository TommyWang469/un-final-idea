Take action

Ask the people who set these rules

Heat shut-off rules are made by state lawmakers and state utility commissions, not Congress. Both answer to the people who live in their state. One personal message from you counts for more than you'd think.

Facing a shut-off right now?

Call your utility today and ask about a payment plan and any help programs. The federal LIHEAP program helps low-income households pay energy bills (find out more), and you can also try dialing 211 for local help. This site is not legal advice.

The short version

  • Know your ask. A new rule, a better trigger, or coverage for everyone.
  • Find who decides. Your state legislators and your state utility commission.
  • Write or meet in person, in your own words. Skip the copy-paste form letter.
  • Follow up. Write down what they said and when to check back.

The steps

  1. Know what your state needs

    Look up your state. The page tells you which of three things to ask for:

    • No rule? Ask for one. A rule that turns on whenever the weather service issues a heat alert covers every dangerous day.
    • A rule with gaps? Ask for a better trigger, like adding heat alerts the way Illinois did.
    • A rule for only some people? Ask for one that covers every household.

    Write down two numbers from your state's page: its heat-alert days a year, and how many of them have no protection. Those are the facts you'll bring.

  2. Find who decides

    Your state legislators can write a rule into law. Find yours by address on Open States.

    Your state utility commission (sometimes called the public service commission) can change the rules for utilities it oversees, often faster than a law. Search for your state's name plus "public utility commission". On its site, look for open cases, called proceedings or dockets, about disconnections or shut-offs.

    Congress matters less here. The Congressional Research Service notes that the federal role in utility shut-offs is limited; states and local regulators run most of it.

  3. Pick a way to reach them

    Start with whichever fits your state. You don't need to do all four.

    Comment to the utility commission

    Commissions take written comments from the public on open cases. Arizona's commission wrote emergency rules within a week in 2019, and California's and New York's recent changes both came through commission cases.

    • California: find the case by its proceeding number, then choose Public Comments, then Add Public Comment. CPUC instructions
    • New York: find the case in the commission's document system, then choose Post Comments. NY PSC instructions
    • Some commissions, California's among them, also hold public hearings where you can sign up to speak.

    Before you submit: your comment may be public, with your name and city. California publishes both unless you choose the private option. Also, don't contact commissioners privately about a case. In some kinds of cases that is not allowed.

    Meet your state legislator

    Email their office to ask for 15 minutes with them or their staff. Say you live in the district. Bring a one-page summary of your state's numbers and one specific ask. A meeting with staff is worth having too.

    Testify at a hearing on a bill

    When a bill is up for a committee hearing, the public can usually speak or send written testimony. Washington State's rules are a clear example: anyone may testify, sign-up closes an hour before the hearing, and written testimony is accepted up to 24 hours after it starts (WA Legislature). Check your own state's rules on its legislature's website.

    Write to your local paper

    A letter to the editor or an opinion piece reaches lawmakers too, especially if it names them. Follow the paper's length rules and tie it to recent news, like a heat wave. When it's published, send it to your legislator's office.

  4. Say it in your own words

    Congressional staff were asked what sways an undecided lawmaker. A visit from a constituent topped the list, at 94%. A copy-paste form email came last, at 56% (Congressional Management Foundation). That survey was about Congress, but the lesson carries over: a short message you wrote yourself beats a long one you didn't.

    Kind of contactStaff saying it has some or a lot of influence
    In-person visit from a constituent94%
    Personal email92%
    Personal letter88%

    From the Congressional Management Foundation's 2015 survey of Congressional staff.

  5. Follow up

    Right after a meeting or call, write down three things: what they said, what they promised, and when you'll check back. Send a thank-you note within two days, with anything you promised to send. Then check back on that date.

What to say, in under two minutes

The anti-poverty group RESULTS teaches a four-part talk called EPIC. It works on paper and in person.

Engage

Who you are and why you care. One sentence.

Problem

The gap in your state, with one number from your state's page.

Inform

The fix, and proof it works somewhere else.

Call to action

A yes-or-no question they can answer, and when you'll follow up.

An example to adapt

I'm a student in your town. Our state has no rule that stops power shut-offs during dangerous heat, and the weather service issued heat alerts on about number from your state's page days a year from 2015 to 2024.

Other states have fixed this. When California lowered its trigger in 2026, its unprotected days dropped from 4.0 to 1.1 a year.

Will you co-sponsor a bill / ask the utility commission to open a rulemaking? When should I follow up?

Tell the truth about your story

A personal reason helps: you live there, you have older relatives, you've sat through a hot summer without cooling. But never say a shut-off happened to you or someone you know if it didn't. One stretched story can sink a good case.

Going with friends?

Split the jobs, as Citizens' Climate Lobby teaches. One person leads and introduces everyone. One makes the ask. One takes notes on what the lawmaker says. One sends the follow-up. Two or three people can share these.

Comment to a utility commission

Keep it to one page, in this order:

  1. The case (proceeding) number and title.
  2. Who you are and where you live.
  3. Your state's current rule, quoted, with a link. Your state's page on this site has both.
  4. The gap, as a number: for example, how many alert days the rule doesn't cover.
  5. The change you want: for example, a heat-index or heat-alert trigger, which the National Consumer Law Center recommends.
  6. Proof it works: California's 2026 change cut unprotected days from 4.0 to 1.1 a year.

Check the privacy setting before you submit.

Written testimony on a bill

Chair name and members of the committee:

My name is name; I live in town; I am testifying for myself in support of bill number.

One sentence, with a source, on the gap in our state. One sentence on what the bill would change. One sentence on a state where a similar rule works, with its number.

I ask the committee to pass bill number. I'm happy to answer questions in writing.

Where the fight is live

These states have no rule, and something is moving or has just failed. If you live in one, your message lands at the right moment. Status as of October 2026.

In conference

Massachusetts

The Senate's version of the energy bill would stop shut-offs for households in financial hardship when it's expected to top 85°F three days in a row, May 15 to September 15. A conference committee is settling the final bill (H5175). The open question: does the heat protection survive? Bill page

Being rewritten

Pennsylvania

The state's utility-customer law lapsed at the end of 2024. Lawmakers are renewing it (SB 154 and HB 2333). A 2024 House version added a summer ban; neither current bill's summary has one. That makes this the time to ask for it as an amendment. Spotlight PA

Died in 2025

Florida

The PAUSE Act died in committee in June 2025. Florida had 524,907 summer shut-offs in 2024, the most of any state without a rule, and 20.2 days a year with a heat index of 105°F or higher. Bill page

Stuck in committee

Vermont

H.88 would stop shut-offs at 92°F or hotter. It hasn't moved since it went to a House committee in January 2025. Bill page

Kansas has the most heat-alert days of any state without a rule (14.2 a year), but we found no bill there. That makes it a place to start from scratch.

Can students do this?

Yes. You don't have to be old enough to vote to contact your lawmakers. Students have free-speech rights, and anyone can petition their government. Washington's legislature, for one, says anyone may testify.

What about lobbying laws? In California, registration covers paid lobbyists and groups that spend $5,000 or more in a quarter, not students volunteering their own time. Other states set their own rules, so check yours if you're unsure.

Two things to keep straight: speak for yourself unless a group has said you can speak for it, and never support or oppose a candidate under our name.
