Why it matters

Losing power in a heat wave can kill. Half the states don't prevent it.

When a family can't pay its electric bill, the utility can cut the power, and with it the air conditioner and the fan. Some states forbid this on dangerously hot days. Many don't.

The short version

  • 24 of 48 states and DC have no rule that stops heat-wave shut-offs.
  • In those 24 states, utilities cut off home electricity 1,412,514 times from June to August 2024.
  • Where rules exist, most work. Two (Delaware and Mississippi) miss three out of four dangerous days.
  • Fixing a rule works. California's 2026 change cut its unprotected days from 4.0 to 1.1 a year.

Shut-offs don't stop for summer

In 2024, US utilities made 13.4 million home electricity disconnections. That is the first federal count. Some homes were cut off more than once, so it counts shut-offs, not households.

  • 1,412,514shut-offs from June to August 2024 in the 24 states with no hot-weather rule.
  • 524,907summer shut-offs in Florida alone, which has no rule. That's about a quarter of its year, so summer slowed nothing down.
  • 3,499summer shut-offs in Arizona, which pauses them in summer. It had 29,023 in December alone.

Nationally, shut-offs don't peak in summer. July 2024 was the lowest month, with 967,012. That dip comes partly from states that already protect people in the heat. Source: US Energy Information Administration, 2024 Residential Utility Disconnections Report.

Many families are one bill away

In 2020, 27% of US households, about 34 million, had trouble paying energy bills or kept their home at an unsafe temperature to save money (EIA). In a later Census survey, 22.4% of adults said they couldn't pay an energy bill (JAMA Network Open).

What happens when the power goes off

Nobody counts how many people are hurt by heat-wave shut-offs. But people have died this way. Two deaths in Arizona are documented.

A $51 balance on a 107°F day

In September 2018, the utility APS cut power to Stephanie Pullman, 72, in Sun City West. She owed $51. The medical examiner found heat exposure, combined with heart disease. Her death led the state's utility commission to adopt a summer rule in 2019. Associated Press

A rule on paper, dropped three days earlier

In May 2024 the same utility cut power to Katherine Korman, 82, on a 99°F day, three days after it dropped its temperature-based heat policy. She was found dead six days later. The utility agreed to a $7 million settlement with the state in 2026. Courthouse News

The second story is the reason this site exists. Having a rule isn't enough. It has to be on, on the day it's hot.

Heat kills more broadly too. Maricopa County, which includes Phoenix, counted 608 heat deaths in 2024 (county report). Its reports don't say how many followed a shut-off, so we don't claim any did.

Rules are changing right now

  1. June 2019 · ArizonaAfter Stephanie Pullman's death became public, the utility commission opened an investigation and adopted emergency rules within a week. KJZZ
  2. 2023 · WashingtonA state law added heat shut-off protections, backed by the Attorney General. WA Attorney General
  3. 2023 · IllinoisThe Attorney General pushed a law that lowered the trigger and added weather alerts. It took effect in 2024. Public Act 103-0019
  4. March 2026 · New YorkThe utility commission set one standard for the major utilities: no shut-offs when the heat index reaches 90°F. NY Public Service Commission
  5. July 2026 · CaliforniaThe utility commission voted 4–0 to lower the trigger from 100°F to 90°F, over the utilities' objections. CalMatters

Every one of these came through one of two doors: state lawmakers or a state utility commission. Those are the same doors you can knock on. Here's how.

What critics say, and how to answer

If you talk to a lawmaker about this, expect these questions. Each has a fair point inside it.

"The bill doesn't go away."

True. A pause delays a shut-off; it doesn't cancel the debt. Arizona's commission says charges keep adding up during its summer pause (ACC). That's why groups like RMI say pauses should come with help paying the bill (RMI). The answer: a pause keeps people alive through the dangerous days, and bill help keeps them out of the cycle.

"Other customers end up paying."

Partly true. Regulators can spread unpaid bills across everyone's rates, and state regulators said the broad COVID-era pauses left big unpaid balances (Congressional Research Service, report R47417). The answer: a heat rule is narrow. In 22 of the 25 states with a rule, it is on fewer than 40 days a year. In the middle state, it's 13.4 days.

"90°F is too broad."

California's utilities argued this in 2026 and lost. Our numbers show why: the old 100°F rule left 4.0 dangerous days a year unprotected; at 90°F it's 1.1.

"This doesn't fix everything."

Correct. Air conditioners break, and many rules don't reach city-owned utilities or co-ops. A shut-off rule closes one way people end up without cooling. It's one proven step, not the whole answer.
