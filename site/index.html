When it is dangerously hot, can your power be cut off?

Some US states bar utilities from disconnecting a household for an unpaid bill during extreme heat. This page counts, state by state, how many of the days the National Weather Service called dangerously hot were actually covered by the state's rule, using each state's own legal text and ten years of weather.

How this was done

  • Rules. Each state's hot-weather rule was read from its statute, regulation or commission order and coded by hand: the trigger (a temperature, a heat index, or a weather-service alert), how far ahead the forecast looks, the season, and who is covered. The source and the quoted text are shown for every state. Where only a secondary source was read, the state is marked.
  • Weather. Daily maximum temperature and heat index from the nearest long-running airport station to the population centre of each state's five most populous counties, 2015 to 2024, weighted by county population.
  • Dangerous days. Days when the National Weather Service had a heat advisory or (excessive) heat warning out for that county's forecast zone. This uses the weather service's own local definition of dangerous heat, which differs by region.
  • Covered. A dangerous day is covered if the state's rule, as written today, would have barred a shut-off on it.

Limits

  • Observed weather stands in for forecasts. Rules act on forecasts, which can miss in either direction.
  • Today's rules, past weather. Several rules changed between 2023 and 2026.
  • Legal protection is not the same as what happened. This does not count actual disconnections.
  • Many rules reach only regulated utilities. Customers of cooperatives and city-owned utilities are often outside them.
  • Five counties per state, measured at airports. Rural areas and neighbourhood heat are under-represented.
  • The list of states with no rule is the weakest input. A state is shown with no rule only where two independent compilations agree. The federal table alone wrongly lists Maine, Minnesota, New York and Oregon as having none.
  • Texas is undercounted and Arizona is scored on its temperature option; see the state notes.
  • This is a student project. It is not legal advice and has not been reviewed by a lawyer or a utility regulator.
