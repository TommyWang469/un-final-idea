How we know

Every number here can be checked

Each state's rule is quoted and linked on its page. All our data and code are public, and our scripts rebuild every weather and shut-off figure on this site. Other facts link to their sources. This is a student project: a lawyer or a utility regulator has not reviewed it.

How we did it

  1. We read each state's rule

    For every state, we wrote down what turns its rule on (a temperature, a heat index, or a weather-service alert), how far ahead it looks at the forecast, which months it covers, and who it protects. 23 of the 25 rules were read from the state's own legal text. Two (Indiana and New York) come from a secondary source and are marked.

  2. We checked which states have no rule

    A state counts as having no rule only where two separate national lists agree: the federal LIHEAP Clearinghouse table and ElectricRates.org's 2026 table. For 16 of those 24 states, we checked further against the state's own code, its regulator, or a news report.

  3. We gathered ten years of weather

    For 2015 to 2024, we took the daily high temperature and heat index from the nearest long-running airport weather station to the five most populous counties in each state, weighted by how many people live in each county.

  4. We marked the dangerous days

    A dangerous day is a day the National Weather Service had a heat advisory or heat warning out for that county, at some point between 9 a.m. and 9 p.m. Days with no weather record are left out. We use the weather service's own local idea of "dangerous", so a heat advisory in Maine is set lower than one in Arizona. That's on purpose.

  5. We checked each day against the rule

    A dangerous day counts as covered if the state's rule, as written today, would have stopped a utility from cutting off a home's power for an unpaid bill that day.

The ZIP code search only finds your state. Results are the same for every ZIP code in a state; they aren't local.

What this can and can't tell you

A rule on paper isn't a shut-off prevented

We count when the law protects people, not what utilities actually did. Many rules apply only to utilities the state regulates; customers of co-ops and city-owned utilities are often outside them.

Real weather stands in for forecasts

Most rules act on a forecast, and forecasts can miss in either direction.

Today's rules, past weather

Several rules changed between 2023 and 2026. We apply today's rule to 2015 to 2024 weather. The shut-off counts are from 2024, before some rules existed.

Airports, not neighborhoods

Readings come from the nearest long-running airport station, never more than 40 miles from a county's center. We use five counties per state, so rural areas and hot city blocks are under-represented.

Heat alerts have become more common

The weather service issued about four times as many heat alerts for these areas in 2024 as in 2015. Some of that is hotter weather; some is the service lowering its bar in places. So a ten-year average understates how often alerts come today.

A few states are simplified
  • Texas is undercounted. Its rule turns on with an alert anywhere in a utility's area; we check only the customer's own area.
  • Arizona is scored on its 95°F option. A utility may instead choose a June 1 to October 15 pause, which protects more days.
  • Oregon's rule also looks 24 hours ahead. We don't model that yet, so its days may be slightly low.
The no-rule list is our weakest input

Eight of the 24 no-rule states rest on the two national lists alone: Michigan, Montana, New Hampshire, North Dakota, South Carolina, South Dakota, Vermont and Wyoming. The 16 we checked further hold 92% of the no-rule group's exposure.

Nobody counts the harm

There is no national count of injuries or deaths from heat-wave shut-offs. We never say "shut-offs kill this many people a year", because no one knows.

Mistakes we caught

We keep these on the record because each one would have produced a confident, wrong claim.

  • The federal table was wrong. It lists Maine, Minnesota, New York and Oregon as having no hot-weather rule. All four have one. It also gets the trigger wrong in several states that do, including Delaware, Oklahoma, Georgia, California and Illinois. Our first quick test trusted that table and passed for the wrong reason.
  • New York almost looked like the second-worst unprotected state. It adopted a rule in March 2026. Checking that turned up the other errors in the federal table.
  • A heat-index shortcut was far too high in humid places. When we checked it against airport records for Miami, Tampa and Oklahoma City, it overcounted dangerous days several times over. Every heat-index figure now comes from station records.
  • An outside code review found seven real bugs, such as counting days with no weather record as unprotected. All seven were fixed. A second review confirmed the fixes and reproduced every headline number. None of the bugs changed the two main conclusions.
  • A page-reading tool gave false answers. It said "no heat provision" for pages that had really sent it to the wrong page or failed to load. We threw out those three results.

Who counted before us

The Energy Justice Lab at Indiana University tracks every state's protections and has counted the days California's old 100°F rule was on. The National Consumer Law Center recommends heat-index or heat-alert triggers. We didn't find a published count, for every state, of how many dangerous days each rule covers. That's the piece we add. It's a small gap, and the experts could close it quickly with better data than ours.

That half the states have no rule was already known. What's new here is how big that gap is in dangerous days, and where it falls.

Sources

  • No-rule list: where the LIHEAP Clearinghouse (US Department of Health and Human Services) and ElectricRates.org's 2026 state table agree, plus our own checks.

  • County populations: 2020 US Census.
